# Project Archimedes: TerraNova's Space-Based Solar Shade Concept

Project Archimedes represents one of TerraNova Geo-Engineering's most ambitious future concepts, exploring the possibility of directly managing incoming solar radiation from space. Managed internally by the highly compartmentalized 'Deep Space Interventions Directorate' (DSID) under 'TN-Aegis Eyes Only' classification, this project envisions a large-scale intervention aimed at subtly reducing the amount of sunlight reaching Earth.

## Concept: A Refractive Lens Constellation at L1

Unlike concepts involving large reflective mirrors, Project Archimedes proposes deploying a vast constellation of *refractive* lenses. These lenses are designed not to block sunlight, but to subtly diverge approximately 1.5% of the incident solar radiation by a fraction of a degree away from the Earth-Sun line. This refractive approach aims to minimize waste heat build-up on the lens structures themselves.

The proposed location for this immense constellation is the Earth-Sun L1 Lagrange point, situated roughly 1.5 million kilometers (930,000 miles) sunward from Earth. This location offers a gravitationally advantageous position relative to the Sun and Earth, but it is inherently unstable. Consequently, the constellation would require constant, active station-keeping to maintain its position against gravitational perturbations and solar wind pressure.

## Scale and Technology

The initial concepts for Project Archimedes are staggering in scale, envisioning a dispersed constellation comprising over 10 million independently controlled lens elements. Collectively, these elements would influence solar radiation across an effective area exceeding 2.5 million square kilometers, achieving the target 1-2% reduction in insolation.

Feasibility studies are evaluating the use of ultra-thin silicon nitride composite films for the individual lens elements. These films could potentially be only 5-10 micrometers thick. To manage the logistics and potential fragility, manufacturing these elements directly in space is being considered.

Each lens element within the constellation would be a sophisticated spacecraft in its own right. They would utilize low-thrust, high-efficiency ion propulsion systems for autonomous navigation, precise station-keeping within the L1 halo orbit, and collision avoidance. These propulsion systems would be powered by integrated thin-film solar arrays on the lens structures.

## Logistics, Feasibility, and Challenges

The logistical requirements for deploying Project Archimedes are immense. TerraNova's analysis indicates the necessity of developing entirely new classes of reusable super-heavy launch vehicles. These vehicles would need the capability to deliver payloads exceeding 200 tonnes directly to the L1 point, potentially requiring the construction of dedicated equatorial launch facilities.

To potentially bypass the prohibitive costs of launching millions of tonnes of material from Earth, TerraNova's 'Blue Sky' research unit is actively modeling an alternative. This involves assessing the economic and technical viability of using lunar regolith as a source for the required silicon. The concept envisions processing this material *in-situ* at a hypothetical fabrication facility located at L1, powered by solar energy.

A comprehensive feasibility study for Project Archimedes was initiated in 2030 and is scheduled for initial completion by 2038. Due to its strategic importance, the study is overseen directly by Dr. Evelyn Reed. Its findings will be crucial for securing long-term funding commitments from Helvetia Climate Solutions AG beyond 2040.

A critical component of this study involves assessing the long-term viability of the constellation. Researchers are evaluating the degradation rates of the lens optics due to cumulative damage from solar particle events (SPEs) and erosion from micrometeoroids prevalent at the L1 point. This assessment aims to project the required replacement and repair cycle for the lens elements over a minimum design life of 50 years.

Project Archimedes remains a long-term, highly complex concept facing enormous technical, logistical, and financial hurdles. Its progression hinges on the outcomes of the ongoing feasibility studies and the potential development of radical new space launch and manufacturing capabilities.
