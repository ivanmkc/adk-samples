# TerraNova's Key Geo-Engineering Projects

TerraNova Geo-Engineering undertakes several large-scale experimental projects aimed at intervening in the Earth's climate system. These initiatives, while aligned with the company's mission of providing "bold solutions for a planet in crisis," are technologically ambitious and subject to significant debate regarding potential unintended consequences. Key projects include stratospheric aerosol injection, ocean fertilization, direct air capture, and advanced climate modeling.

## Project Nimbus: Stratospheric Aerosol Injection

One of TerraNova's flagship initiatives is 'Project Nimbus', which experiments with stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) techniques designed to reflect a portion of incoming sunlight back into space, thereby inducing a cooling effect. The primary operational base for Project Nimbus is a remote TerraNova facility located near Kiruna, Sweden.

From this site, high-altitude drone fleets, designated 'StratoCruisers', are launched to conduct SAI tests. These drones disperse aerosols at altitudes ranging between 18 and 22 kilometers. Project Nimbus focuses on testing calcium carbonate particles, chosen for their potentially lower impact on the ozone layer compared to traditional sulfate aerosols. Following initial smaller pilot tests commencing in 2025, the full-scale experimental phases for Project Nimbus began in 2028.

## Oceanus Carbon Sink: Ocean Fertilization

The 'Oceanus Carbon Sink' project aims to enhance the ocean's natural capacity to absorb atmospheric carbon dioxide. TerraNova's primary test zone for this project is strategically situated within the nutrient-poor South Pacific Gyre, approximately 1,500 kilometers east of New Zealand.

Early experiments under Oceanus involved the controlled dispersal of iron sulfate, a key limiting nutrient for phytoplankton growth in this region. Trials saw up to 10 tonnes of iron sulfate distributed across 300 square kilometer areas of the ocean surface. The objective is to trigger localized phytoplankton blooms, which absorb CO2 during photosynthesis. To monitor the effects and track the induced blooms, TerraNova employs a fleet of autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs), nicknamed 'Plankton Trackers'. These AUVs are equipped with specialized instruments, including fluorometers and nutrient sensors, to gather data on the experiments' impact.

## Atmospheric Reclaimers: Direct Air Capture

TerraNova also operates large-scale direct air capture facilities known as 'Atmospheric Reclaimers', designed to remove CO2 directly from the ambient air. The largest of these facilities, codenamed 'Site Boreas', is located in Iceland. This location was chosen strategically to leverage the region's abundant geothermal energy resources, providing a low-carbon power source for the energy-intensive capture process.

'Site Boreas' utilizes a liquid solvent-based capture system, specifically employing a potassium hydroxide solution to chemically bind with atmospheric CO2. The facility has an ambitious target capacity of removing 1 million tonnes of CO2 from the atmosphere annually. The captured CO2 is primarily sequestered through a mineralization process, which involves injecting it into nearby underground basalt rock formations, where it reacts to form stable carbonate minerals.

## AI and Predictive Modeling

Underpinning these large-scale interventions is TerraNova's significant investment in advanced atmospheric modeling and Artificial Intelligence (AI). The company's AI division, led by Chief Scientist Dr. Kenji Tanaka, has developed a sophisticated predictive modeling suite known as 'ClimateSim'. This suite is crucial for forecasting the potential regional and global impacts of TerraNova's geo-engineering activities, aiming to anticipate consequences and refine experimental parameters before and during deployment.
